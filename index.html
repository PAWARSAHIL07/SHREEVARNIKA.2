When the software you're testing ends up in an operating theatre, "it works on my machine" stops being a joke. On the surgical-technology accounts I support, I help validate computerized systems that assist surgeons — and the first thing I unlearned was treating testing as a phase. Here, it's closer to a legal act.

Every test I execute is scripted, evidenced and traceable. A requirement walks forward into a risk, the risk into a test case, the test case into a result, the result into a signed summary — and the whole chain has to survive an auditor reading it backwards. That's what 21 CFR Part 11 really means in practice: the electronic record is as real as the product itself.

People sometimes imagine medical-device testing is slow because regulators enjoy paperwork. It's slow because patients can't file defect tickets. Somewhere along the way I stopped fighting that pace and started respecting it — slow is smooth, and smooth is fast. Patience, it turns out, was a job skill all along.

Most test plans start by asking "what should we test?". In medical devices, the question is "what could hurt someone, and how much proof do we need that it won't?". ISO 14971 flips testing from coverage-driven to risk-driven — and once that clicks, there's no going back.

In practice: every hazard gets a severity, a probability, and a detection question. A failure that could reach a patient earns deeper verification than a cosmetic glitch on a screen nobody looks at. When I write a validation plan now, the risk file should read like a preview of what I'm about to test. If it doesn't, the plan is wrong — and I'd rather find that out myself than have an auditor find it for me.

The uncomfortable truth I've made peace with: "we tested everything" is not an answer anyone should accept, including me. Finite effort belongs where harm lives. Arguing with your own test plan isn't self-doubt — it's the job.

People assume Six Sigma and computerized system validation come from different worlds — one born on manufacturing floors, the other in regulated labs. I don't buy it. Both start from the same refusal: accepting defects as normal.

DMAIC — Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control — is really just disciplined curiosity. Define what "good" means, measure what's actually happening, chase the root cause instead of the symptom, improve deliberately, then control so the fix stays fixed. Validation does the same dance in different vocabulary: requirements define, testing measures, risk analysis analyzes, CAPA improves, periodic review controls.

The Green Belt gave me a shared language with process and manufacturing teams — and a sharper instinct for asking "why" five times before patching anything. In a regulated environment that matters double: you can't just hot-fix a validated system and move on. Whatever you call it — Six Sigma or GAMP 5 — quality is ultimately the habit of not letting things slide.
